# Performance case studies

Six before-and-after studies on a **1,165,043 row partitioned fact**, every number
measured on the live database and captured by [`benchmark.py`](benchmark.py).

> **All data is synthetic.** It is generated by [`generate_data.py`](generate_data.py) from the
> fixed seed `dealflow-2026-v1`. No real broker, agency, property, deal or client appears
> anywhere in this repository. The timings are real measurements on that generated data.

The SQL lives in [`sql/06_performance.sql`](sql/06_performance.sql) and nowhere else.
`benchmark.py` parses the `-- @block` directives out of that file and executes them, so
the harness and the SQL cannot drift apart: there is exactly one copy of every query and
every index. Raw output of all five runs quoted below is in
[`plans/benchmark_run.txt`](plans/benchmark_run.txt), and every `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)`
plan is in [`plans/`](plans/).

Reproduce with `./run.sh benchmark`. It is safe to run repeatedly: the fixture is gated by
a source-checksum provenance check, so a second run either reuses a fixture it has proved
matches the warehouse or rebuilds it from scratch.

## Results

Ranges are across **five runs of the whole suite**, each the median of 5 timed runs after one
warmup. Raw output of every run is in
[`plans/benchmark_run.txt`](plans/benchmark_run.txt).

| Case | What was wrong | Before | After | Speedup, 5 runs | Quote this |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | Non-sargable month filter defeats partition pruning | 215 to 448 ms | 14 to 26 ms | 12x to 33x | **~12x** |
| 2 | A broker performance review has no access path | 105 to 141 ms | 4.7 to 15 ms | 8.7x to 26x | **~8.7x** |
| 3 | Dwell percentiles sort 1.17M rows with no ordered path | 5.6 to 7.7 s | 4.1 to 4.8 s | 1.3x to 1.7x | **~1.3x** |
| 4 | Open pipeline reads every deal to report on 4% of them | 22 to 47 ms | 5.0 to 10 ms | 3.1x to 7.3x | **~3.1x** |
| 5 | BRIN on the partitioned fact | 111 to 157 ms | 18 to 28 ms | 5.4x to 7.0x | **~5.4x** |
| 5b | The free rewrite, head to head against that BRIN | 16 to 43 ms | 3.9 to 5.8 ms | 3.1x to 9.9x | **~3.1x** |

The last column is the **lowest speedup seen on any of the five runs**, and it is the number to
quote. A range whose top end is three times its bottom end does not support a decimal place, so
none is given.

### Why the ranges are that wide, and what is actually stable

Five consecutive runs, no manual reset between them, every one exit 0. That is also the
re-runnability check: the fixture is cached behind a source-checksum gate, and all five reused a
fixture they had proved matched the warehouse. Per-run speedups:

| Case | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 | Run 4 | Run 5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 11.9x | **32.6x** | 15.5x | 13.3x | 21.3x |
| 2 | 15.4x | 15.6x | 20.5x | 8.7x | 25.7x |
| 3 | 1.3x | 1.3x | 1.4x | 1.7x | 1.6x |
| 4 | 3.1x | 7.3x | 3.4x | 3.2x | 4.3x |
| 5 | 6.5x | 5.7x | 7.0x | 6.3x | 5.4x |
| 5b | 4.8x | **9.9x** | 3.1x | 4.4x | 4.9x |

**The two bolded runs are the ones to distrust, and the harness said so at the time.** Run 2's
case 1 *before* had a min-median-max of 435.6 / 447.9 / 901.9 ms and its case 5b *before* spread
25.6 / 43.0 / 320.7 ms, which the harness flagged as `max/min 12.5x, treat with suspicion`. Run 4
was flagged the same way on cases 1 and 2. **Nothing about the fix improved on those runs. The
un-indexed baseline got worse**, and a slow baseline is how a benchmark flatters a fix. Two of
five runs carrying a spread warning on a laptop container is the honest state of this
measurement, and it is exactly why the quoted column is the minimum rather than the best or the
mean.

**And the minimum is not a floor. Two further runs were taken after this table was written, and
one of them came in below it.** That run measured case 5 at **2.9x**, against the 5.4x quoted
above. The harness flagged it: the case 5 *after* spread was 21.3 / 46.6 / 66.2 ms, `max/min
3.1x, treat with suspicion`, and it is the *after* side that wandered, not the baseline. The
plan did not change at all on that run: 85 partitions, 665 buffers, byte identical to every
other run. **So the correct reading of the "quote this" column is "the lowest of five runs on
this box", not "a bound".** A wall clock figure from a laptop container is not a bound and this
document should not pretend otherwise. The buffer counts are the part that behaves like one.

Timings are client-observed round trip inside one already open session, so **planning time
is included**. That is deliberate: planning time is part of what a user waits for, and case
1 turns out to be as much a planning win as an execution win.

### Measurement honesty

**The plan facts reproduce. The wall clock does not.** This is the single most useful thing in
this document. Over the same five runs whose speedups moved by 3x, these came back **byte
identical every single time**:

| Evidence | Before | After | Runs identical |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Case 1 fact partitions in the plan | **85** | **3** | 5 of 5 |
| Case 1 buffers | 21,220 read + 54 hit | 1,098 hit, 0 read | 5 of 5 |
| Case 2 fact partitions in the plan | 85 | 85 | 5 of 5 |
| Case 2 buffers | 21,222 read + 171 hit | **1,409 hit**, 0 read | 5 of 5 |
| Case 3 temp blocks written | 20,713 | **8,706** | 5 of 5 |
| Case 4 buffers | 33,965 | **202** | 5 of 5 |
| Case 5b partitions, BRIN / rewrite | 85 | **1** | 5 of 5 |
| Case 5b buffers, rewrite side | | **432** | 5 of 5 |
| Case 5b buffers, BRIN side | 665 | | 3 of 5 (765 and 771 on the other two) |

**The one figure in that table that is not perfectly stable is the BRIN one, and the direction
matters.** The rewrite reads exactly 432 buffers on every run because partition pruning is
deterministic: one partition, the same pages. The BRIN side reads 665 buffers three times out of
five and 765 or 771 the other two, because BRIN range exclusion depends on how the summaries
happen to line up against the block ranges after the index is rebuilt. The stable side is the
one that wins, which is a small point in its own favour.

`EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` is captured **separately** from the timings, for plan shape and
buffer counts only, because its per-node instrumentation is not free. Measured on this build it
is modest: case 4's *after* query came out at 9.4 ms on the clock against 8.4 ms of execution
self-reported by `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` plus 0.2 ms of planning, and case 3's *after* at 4,290.6 ms on
the clock against 4,411.6 ms self-reported. **Shape from EXPLAIN, speed from the clock**, and the
reason is the direction of the bias rather than its size.

The harness reports min / median / max for every case and counts competing backends during each
run. **It flagged two of the five runs**, both for a spread wide enough to distrust rather than
for a competing backend, and those are the two called out above. No competing backends were seen
on any run. An earlier run of the suite, taken while a warehouse load was running in the same
container, reported case 3's *before* at 12,173 ms against 2,830 ms on a quiet box, which is why
that check exists at all.

### Container under test

PostgreSQL 16.14 on aarch64, 8 vCPU, `shared_buffers=128MB`, `work_mem=4MB`,
`max_parallel_workers_per_gather=2`. The small `work_mem` is the container default and is
left alone on purpose: it is what makes the disk spill in case 3 a real observation rather
than a manufactured one.

The lab lives in its own `perf` schema, built **from `mart`** and verified against it at
run time (same row count, same value distribution, same physical ordering, and since a
`dim_date` column was once wrong under a correct heading, the same calendar column by column).
Measuring a *before* state means dropping the exact indexes the warehouse depends on, and doing
that to a loaded `mart` would mutate a production object mid-flight and reset the index usage
counters that `mart`'s own index justification artifact reads.

`perf.fct_deal_pipeline` holds **248,702** rows against `mart.fct_deal_pipeline`'s 248,940,
and the difference is not a defect: the lab's snapshot is aggregated from stage events, so
the 238 deals that carry no stage event have no row in it.

## Case 1. Non-sargable predicate

**Question.** For the first quarter of 2026, how many deals moved between each pair of
stages, and how long did they sit in the previous stage?

**Before.**

```sql
WHERE to_char(e.event_ts, 'YYYY-MM') IN ('2026-01', '2026-02', '2026-03')
```

**Diagnosis.** The filter wraps the timestamp in `to_char()` and compares the result to
text. The planner cannot reason about a function result against partition boundaries, so it
prunes nothing:

| | before | after |
|---|---:|---:|
| Fact partitions in the plan | **85** | **3** |
| Planning time | 2.6 to 32.0 ms | 0.2 to 0.4 ms |
| Buffers | 21,220 read + 54 hit | 1,098 hit, 0 read |

Partition count and buffers were byte identical on every run. Planning time is given as
the range because it is the figure that moved, and the 32.0 ms top end came from the run the
harness independently flagged as noisy.

**Fix.** A half-open range on the partition key itself. No DDL, no index, no new object.

```sql
WHERE e.event_date >= DATE '2026-01-01'
  AND e.event_date <  DATE '2026-04-01'
```

**Why half-open and not `BETWEEN`.** `BETWEEN` on a timestamp silently drops or double
counts the boundary day, depending on whether the upper bound carries a time component.
Half-open ranges compose: the next quarter starts exactly where this one ends.

### The cost side of partitioning

**This is the one place in the repository these figures live.** Everywhere else links here,
because the same measurement previously appeared in four files with four different answers.

The planner must consider every partition before it can prune, so planning time rises with
partition count. Three cases, each measured three times in a **fresh session** plus the value in
the checked-in evidence file:

| Case | Fresh session, 3 reps | Evidence file | Warm re-run in the same session |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Pruned at plan time (literal bounds) | 1.06 / 1.08 / 1.79 ms | 2.58 ms ([`710`](plans/evidence_710_pruning_plan_time.txt)) | 0.94 ms |
| Nothing prunes (no date predicate) | 9.96 / 10.87 / 21.73 ms | 9.01 ms ([`730`](plans/evidence_730_no_pruning.txt)) | 7.03 ms |
| Generic parameterised plan, all 85 carried | 28.37 / 36.54 / 38.30 ms | 28.82 ms ([`720`](plans/evidence_720_pruning_run_time.txt)) | 1.5 to 4.4 ms |

**Read the last row across, because it is the finding.** A generic plan cannot see the parameter
at plan time, so it carries all 85 partitions into the plan and eliminates 82 of them during
execution (`Subplans Removed: 82`). In a fresh session that costs **28 to 38 ms of planning**,
roughly 20 to 30 times the pruned case. Run it a second time in the same session and it collapses
to a few milliseconds, because that cost is almost entirely **catalogue lookups**: the plan reads
about 7,500 catalogue buffers the first time and finds them cached afterwards. So the honest
statement is not one number, it is a shape. **The first statement in a connection pays for the
partition count; the tenth does not.** On a warehouse queried by short-lived connections, the
first statement is most of them.

An order of magnitude of planning time on the case that cannot prune, paid before any row is
read, is why the partition count is capped in the low hundreds and why daily partitioning, which
would mean about 2,900 of them, was rejected. The figures above are for 85.

## Case 2. Missing access path

**Question.** For one broker, across their whole history, how many stage events and what
value?

**Diagnosis.** No index leads with `broker_sk`, so all 85 partitions are sequentially
scanned and 1,165,043 rows are filtered down to the **1,088** that belong to the broker, which
is 0.093 percent of the fact.

**Fix.** `CREATE INDEX ix_fct_event_broker_date ON ... (broker_sk, event_date)`.

| | before | after |
|---|---:|---:|
| Buffers | 21,222 read + 171 hit | **1,409 hit**, 0 read |
| Fact partitions in the plan | 85 | 85 |

Both rows were byte identical on all five runs. A 15x reduction in buffers, and every one of
the remaining 1,409 is a cache hit rather than a read.

**"One broker" means all of their versions, and getting that wrong is the interesting part of
this case.** It originally read `WHERE e.broker_sk = 407`. On a Type 2 fact `broker_sk` is a
**version** key, not a person key: 407 was one *closed* version of broker `BRK-000270`, valid
from `-infinity` to 2020-11-01, and that broker had a second version still open. So the query
held 114 of that broker's 948 events, returned a single row for the year 2020, and called it a
career. The headline selectivity that made this case look dramatic, 0.0098 percent, was an
artifact of having picked a ten-month stub. The filter now resolves every version of one
natural key, which is the honest form of the question and still has no access path without the
index. Which broker is decided at fixture build time and recorded in
`perf.fixture_provenance`, so nothing depends on an identity-assigned number that a warehouse
rebuild can move. Full write-up: [`docs/defects.md` #8](docs/defects.md).

The *after* plan still carries all **85** partitions, exactly as the *before* plan does: the
index is created on the partitioned parent, so every partition contributes its own index scan.
Nothing was pruned, because the filter is not on the partition key. What changed is the cost of
each of those 85 visits, from a share of a sequential scan to a handful of buffers. **Indexing
and pruning are solving different problems**, and this case is the cleanest place in the file to
see that: case 1 cuts the partition count and leaves the access method alone, case 2 does the
opposite.

**Why that column order.** `broker_sk` is the equality predicate so it must lead;
`event_date` is the range. A composite btree can only use a range predicate on the column
*after* the last equality. Reversed, the index would be a worse copy of partition pruning.

## Case 3. The case that only partly worked

This is the most useful study in the file, because the obvious fix mostly did not pay off
and the report says so.

**Question.** Across all history, what is the median and 90th percentile number of days a
deal spends in each stage?

**Diagnosis.** Two separate sorts, both spilling to disk. The `LAG` window needs its input
ordered by `(deal_nk, event_ts)` and nothing provides that order, so the planner sorts all
1,165,043 rows: `Sort Method: external merge Disk: 47984kB`. `percentile_cont` then needs
its own sort of the same rows by dwell value: another `external merge Disk: 28448kB`.

**Fix.** A btree on `(deal_nk, event_ts)`. `Merge Append` reads the 85 partitions in key
order, so the window receives a pre-sorted stream and its 48 MB sort **disappears
entirely**.

| | before | after |
|---|---:|---:|
| Temp blocks written | 20,713 | **8,706** |
| Window step sort | external merge, 47,984 kB | gone, fed by `Merge Append` |
| Percentile sort | external merge, 28,448 kB | external merge, 28,448 kB |
| Median wall clock | 5,648 to 7,663 ms | 4,122 to 4,771 ms |

Every row of that table except the last was byte identical on every run.

**The honest conclusion.** The plan improvement is unambiguous and the clock improvement is
real but small, against a 2.4x reduction in temp blocks written. `percentile_cont` has to sort
its whole input to find a percentile; that is inherent to the aggregate and no index can remove
it, so the 28 MB spill stays. Worse, `Merge Append` over an index gives up the parallel
sequential scan and trades it for random heap access: the *after* plan touches **1,127,596**
shared buffers (1,099,266 hits plus 28,330 reads in
[`plans/case3_after.txt`](plans/case3_after.txt)) against the *before* plan's 21,221, and it
wins anyway only because almost all of that is a cache hit on a 166 MB fact inside a container
that has more memory than that. That total is the same 1,127,596 on every run; only the split
between hits and reads shifts by a handful.

**At what point does that trade invert, and how would I find out?** Not by reasoning about it.
Drop `shared_buffers` below the size of the fact, or grow the fact past available memory, and
re-run the same two plans: those cache hits become random reads and the sequential scan the
planner gave up starts to look much better. The harness can do that; I have not done it, and
that is the honest state of this answer. An earlier run on a loaded laptop did report this case
**net slower**, which did not recur in the runs recorded here. A report that quoted only the
plan, or only its best run, would be misleading.

**What actually fixes this question:** not an index. Materialise the aggregate. That is why
[`mart.mv_broker_month`](sql/04_facts.sql) exists.

## Case 4. Partial index and index-only scan

**Question.** Which open deals in late-funnel stages carry the most value at risk?

**Diagnosis.** The query reads all **248,702** rows of a table that accumulates forever to
report on the **9,838** that are both open and sitting in stages 3 to 7, which is 4.0 percent
of it. (Open deals alone are 14,694, or 5.9 percent; the stage filter takes it to 4.0.) The
plan shows `Rows Removed by Filter: 79,621` per worker.

**Fix.** A partial index on the open subset, with the returned columns in `INCLUDE`.

| | before | after |
|---|---:|---:|
| Buffers | 33,965 | **202** |
| Heap fetches | n/a | **0** (index-only scan) |
| Index size | 12 MB (full equivalent) | **728 kB** (partial) |

Two separate wins, worth separating because they are different techniques:

- **`WHERE is_open`** makes the index track the working set instead of growing with
  history. Measured **16.5x** smaller than the full-column equivalent, 745,472 bytes against
  12,320,768
  ([`plans/evidence_820_partial_index_size.txt`](plans/evidence_820_partial_index_size.txt)).
- **`INCLUDE`** puts the returned-but-not-searched columns in the leaf pages, so the query
  never touches the heap at all. `Heap Fetches: 0` is the proof, and it is conditional: an
  index-only scan still has to consult the heap for any page not marked all-visible in the
  **visibility map**, and that map is maintained by `VACUUM`, not by `ANALYZE`. On this fixture
  autovacuum does it after the bulk load, and `pg_class` confirms it: `relallvisible` 4,431 of
  `relpages` 4,431. **So a freshly loaded table can report non-zero heap fetches until vacuum
  catches up**, and the index is not the thing that changed. Worth knowing before blaming an
  index for a plan that looked better yesterday.

## Case 5. BRIN, measured and then rejected

BRIN stores one min/max summary per block range instead of one entry per row. That is
enormously cheaper, but only useful when the indexed column correlates with physical row
position.

**On the unpartitioned landing table, BRIN is the right answer. Two measurements, and the
second one is the one that matters:**

| Table | `ingested_at` | Distinct values | BRIN | btree | Ratio |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `perf.raw_stage_event` (lab mirror) | copied from `event_ts` | 1,152,758 of 1,165,043 | 24 kB | 25 MB | 1,060x |
| `raw.stage_event` (**what ships**) | real batch stamp | **79** of 1,204,742 | 24 kB | **8,200 kB** | **342x** |

The lab mirror fills `ingested_at` from the event timestamp
([`sql/06_performance.sql`](sql/06_performance.sql), block `130_raw_mirror`), so it is
near-unique, which is exactly the case where a btree is at its largest. **The real landing
table is the one the design decision governs**, and a real batch ingest stamps a whole file at
once: 79 distinct timestamps across 1.2 million rows. btree deduplication collapses repeated
keys into posting lists, so the btree is 8,200 kB rather than 25 MB.

So the honest ratio is **342x**, not 1,060x. BRIN wins either way and the decision was right
either way, which is precisely what would have made leaving the 3x inflation unstated worse
rather than harmless: nobody would have caught it, and it would have come apart on one
follow-up question about what a real ingest timestamp looks like.

The planner chose the BRIN (`Bitmap Index Scan on ix_raw_ingested_brin`) on the lab mirror.
Evidence: [`plans/evidence_810_brin_on_raw.txt`](plans/evidence_810_brin_on_raw.txt). The
8,200 kB figure is reproducible in one statement:

```sql
CREATE INDEX tmp_btree_ingested ON raw.stage_event (ingested_at);
SELECT pg_size_pretty(pg_relation_size('raw.tmp_btree_ingested'))     AS btree,   -- 8200 kB
       pg_size_pretty(pg_relation_size('raw.ix_raw_stage_event_brin')) AS brin;    -- 24 kB
DROP INDEX raw.tmp_btree_ingested;
```

This is why `sql/01_staging.sql` puts BRIN on all four landing tables and nothing else.

**On the partitioned fact, BRIN was built, measured and deleted.** It does help a
timestamp-range query (111 to 157 ms down to 18 to 28 ms across five runs), but case 5b puts it
head to head against the *free* rewrite that filters on the partition key instead:

| | Median | Partitions in plan | Buffers |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| With the BRIN index | 16 to 43 ms | 85 | 665, and 765 or 771 on two of five runs |
| Rewrite on the partition key, no index | **3.9 to 5.8 ms** | **1** | **432 on every run** |

Faster **with no new object to build, maintain or vacuum**. The partition counts and both
buffer figures were identical on every run, which is why they are the numbers quoted here; the
speedup range is in the results table at the top. The arithmetic explains it: the average
partition is **292 pages** and the largest is 505, so at the default 128 `pages_per_range` a
partition holds two to four BRIN ranges and there is very little a summary can exclude. 85 BRIN
indexes across the fact cost 2,040 kB
([`plans/evidence_830_brin_size_on_fact.txt`](plans/evidence_830_brin_size_on_fact.txt))
to be slower than free.

**The rewrite changes the predicate, so it has to prove it did not change the answer.**
`event_ts >= TIMESTAMPTZ '2026-03-01'` and `event_date >= DATE '2026-03-01'` are only
interchangeable because `event_date` is **derived from** `event_ts` under a pinned reporting
timezone (`sql/01_staging.sql` step 0). Under an unpinned timezone the two predicates select
different rows either side of a month boundary, which is exactly why the zone is pinned on the
database rather than described in a README. Case 5b's setup now runs both predicates and raises
if the counts or sums ever differ, so the equivalence is proved on every run instead of being
asserted once in prose.

**The transferable lesson:** partition pruning and BRIN solve the same problem, and if you
already have declarative partitioning on the column, BRIN on that column is redundant. Fix
the query before you add an index.

## Two more measured findings

**Extended statistics belong on the partition, never on the parent.** `from_stage_sk` and
`to_stage_sk` are near functionally dependent (deals move forward one stage at a time), so
without extended statistics the planner multiplies two independent selectivities and badly
underestimates every funnel query. A bad row estimate is what flips a hash join to a nested
loop **at a volume where that choice matters**, and the second half of that sentence is doing
real work: see the limit stated below the table.

The clean A/B is already sitting in this database, because `mart`'s partitions carry a
statistics object and the `perf` mirror's partitions carry none. Same data, same partition,
same predicate (`from_stage_sk = 1 AND to_stage_sk = 2` on the February 2026 partition,
actual 4,497 rows):

| | Estimated rows | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| No extended statistics (`perf` partition) | 1,136 | 4,497 |
| Object on the **parent**, `ANALYZE` on the parent | 1,136 | 4,497 |
| Object on the **partition** (`mart`) | **4,497** | 4,497 |

Without the object the planner is out by 4.0x. An object on the parent, accepted by
PostgreSQL and populated by `ANALYZE`, changes the per-partition estimate by **nothing at
all**: 1,136 before and 1,136 after, because the planner estimates each partition from that
partition's own statistics. The per-partition object lands the estimate exactly, and it needs
all three kinds to do so, `(ndistinct, dependencies, mcv)`, which is what
`util.fn_ensure_month_partition` creates. Recreating it with only `(ndistinct, dependencies)`
got to 2,094: better than 1,136, still out by 2.1x, and `mcv` is what closes the gap. `DQ-008`
fails the load if a populated partition is missing its object, and `ASSERT-WH-029` reports the
same thing in the assertion battery.

**The limit, stated rather than implied. The estimate improved; the plan shape did not.** That
partition holds 19,406 rows, which is small enough that a hash join and a nested loop both
finish in milliseconds, so nothing in the plan flipped as a result of the 4x better estimate.
This document does not have a before-and-after plan showing a join method change, because on
this data there is not one to show. What would have to be true for the flip: the underestimated
row count times the inner probe cost would have to exceed the hash build, which needs either a
much larger inner relation or an error much worse than 4x. That has not been reproduced here.
Naming the limit is a stronger claim than implying a win that was not observed.

**`ADD CONSTRAINT ... NOT VALID` does not work on a partitioned parent.** The standard
low-lock pattern for adding a foreign key to a large table is `NOT VALID` followed by
`VALIDATE CONSTRAINT`. PostgreSQL 16 refuses it on a partitioned parent:

```
ERROR:  cannot add NOT VALID foreign key on partitioned table "fct_deal_stage_event"
        referencing relation "dim_property"
DETAIL:  This feature is not yet supported on partitioned tables.
```

`NOT VALID` does work on an individual leaf partition, which is a plain table: measured **3.3
ms to add plus 1.9 ms to validate** on the 19,406 row February 2026 partition. But the plain
validating `ADD CONSTRAINT` on the parent, across all 85 partitions and 1,165,043 rows, is
cheap enough that the workaround is not worth it: **117 to 344 ms for one key** over four
samples (the 344 ms was the first, on a cold cache), and **1.8 to 2.9 s for all five keys
together**, which is what the fixture's `100_fact_foreign_keys` block reports on every run.

So the per-partition workaround would buy a shorter lock on each of 85 tables in exchange for
85 separate DDL statements, no constraint on the parent, and nothing to show for it at this
volume. Under two seconds of exclusive lock for referential integrity across the whole fact is
worth taking. The figure to carry forward is the shape of it: this cost grows with row count,
so measure it at your volume before a maintenance window, not during one.
